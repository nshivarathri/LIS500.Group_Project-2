LIS 500: Group Reflection

One of the questions we kept coming back to in LIS 500 was what it means for a technology to “work.” In Joy Buolamwini’s Unmasking AI, the example that stuck with us was how the software detected her face only after she put on a white mask. We thought about how easy it would be for someone whose face was detected right away to assume the software worked for everyone. It made us realize that who we test a system with shapes what problems we get a chance to notice.

We had a chance to engage with the ideas that Buolamwini presented through our Teachable Machine project, where we built a model to recognize English, Hindi, and Telugu handwriting. Some of our Hindi examples came from old high school assignments, and writing out individual letters of the alphabet made us pay closer attention to what the model was seeing. If many of those letters came from the same kind of lined paper, could the model pick up on the paper too? Building the website raised a different question. We could display the model’s confidence score, but we also had to think about what someone trying the site for the first time would take that number to mean.

Working with students from different majors was also an important part of the experience. We each spent time on different parts of the project, including the model, website, video, and results. When we brought everything together, we had to explain our choices to teammates who had been focused on a different part of the work. That helped us see where we were assuming that something was obvious, like what a confidence score meant or what our examples could tell us about how the model would perform on someone else’s handwriting.

The weekly discussions about AI and creativity gave us another way to think about what we might miss when we focus on a finished result. The film’s discussion of Aboriginal art stood out because it showed how a piece can carry a community’s history and pass stories down across generations. We could find an AI-generated image visually interesting and still ask what we were leaving out if we judged it by appearance alone.

Across the readings, discussions, and projects, we got into the habit of asking whose experiences are represented, what our results leave out, and how someone with a different perspective might understand what we made. That’s something we can take into future classes and projects, especially while there’s still time to change what we’re building.
